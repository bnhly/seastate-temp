How it works

We built Sea State Explorer because getting a quick, honest answer to "what's the sea like out there in July?" was harder than it should be. The data exists, but it's buried in research archives. This page explains where our numbers come from and how far you can trust them.

Where the numbers come from

None of this is measured at your point, and none of it is a forecast. It comes from reanalysis, which is what you get when forecasters re-run their weather and wave models over past decades and keep them honest with satellite and buoy observations. The result is a record every 3 hours, everywhere, going back to 1980. Most metocean studies start from the same kind of data.

Here's what we use:

WhatSource
WavesWAVERYS wave reanalysis from the Copernicus Marine Service. Grid cells about 20 km apart, every 3 hours, 1980 to 2023.
WindERA5 reanalysis from the Copernicus Climate Change Service (ECMWF). Coarser grid, about 50 km, up to 2024.

When you click, we use the closest grid cell and tell you how far away it is. We don't blend neighbouring cells together. If you want the exact record behind a result, open Data sources at the bottom of the explorer.

Reading the results

Percent of time above a wave height

The big number at the top is how often significant wave height (Hs) was at or above your limit, counting every 3 hourly record in the months you picked. Hs is roughly the average of the highest third of the waves, so expect the odd wave close to double that.

Extremes

When we say "1 sea state in 100 is above 6 m", we mean 1 in every 100 records was higher than 6 m. That's not a 100 year wave, and it shouldn't be treated like one. If you need design extremes, you need a proper extreme value study.

Weather windows

This counts how many separate working periods of your chosen length fit into a typical month with Hs below your limit. A three day calm counts as three 24 hour periods. We estimate it from how long calm spells usually last rather than counting them one by one, and when we tested it against exact counts it was usually within one period a month. Good enough for planning, not for a contract.

Across the day

Average wind and wave height for each 3 hour slot of the day. The times are local solar time, so noon is when the sun is highest, which may not match your clock. Near the coast and in the tropics you'll often see a calm morning and a breezy afternoon. Out in open ocean there's usually no pattern at all, and the explorer will tell you that.

Current and tide

You get the typical peak tidal stream at springs and neaps, how long slack water lasts, and the background current that runs regardless of the tide. The tide model only gives an average over the full water depth, so we scale it to the surface and seabed using the usual 1/7 power profile. The combined table adds tide, wind and background current together following DNVGL-RP-C205 section 4.1. Use it to see whether current matters at a site. It isn't a design current.

Tropical cyclones

We count how often tropical storms and cyclones have come within 120, 250, 500 and 1,000 nautical miles of your point in the months you picked, and show the strongest on record. Plenty of operations start securing and demobilising once a cyclone is inside 1,000 nm, so expect that downtime on top of the wave statistics.

Assets

Platforms, wells, fields and pipelines come from public registers and are there to help you get your bearings. The positions are approximate. Don't navigate by them.

Where it falls short

The models are coarse, so treat anything close to land, in a bay, or in shallow or sheltered water with suspicion. They also smooth out tropical cyclones and tend to understate the worst seas. With a record every 3 hours, short sharp events can slip between readings.

Wave height is only part of the story. Whether a job can go ahead also depends on wave period, direction, current, and how your vessel actually moves. And none of this is for engineering design, navigation, or deciding go or no-go on the day.

Sharing

Every result has its own link. Hit Copy link to this result and whoever opens it lands on the same point with the same months and limits. The PDF summary has the link on it too.

Please don't scrape the data in bulk. The open datasets underneath are free from the providers listed below.

Sea State Explorer is published by Thrust Maritime. These are long term climatological statistics and not a forecast. They are likely to underestimate extremes, especially close to coastlines, in sheltered or shallow water and in tropical cyclone conditions. The data is free for everyone to use, but it is not warranted by Thrust Maritime: use it at your own risk.
