How it works

Click a point at sea, pick the months you care about, and the explorer tells you how that patch of sea has behaved over more than forty years. This page covers where the numbers come from, how to read them, and where they stop being useful.

1. Where the numbers come from

Nothing here is measured at your point and nothing is a forecast. The figures come from reanalysis: weather and wave models re-run over past decades, kept on track by satellite and buoy observations, and saved every 3 hours on a global grid. It is the same kind of data that early stage metocean studies start from.

The explorer uses the nearest grid cell to your click and tells you how far away it is. It does not blend neighbouring cells. The Data sources table at the foot of the explorer always shows the exact record in use.

2. Reading the results

Percent of time above a wave height

The headline figure is the share of all 3 hourly records, in the months you picked, where significant wave height (Hs) was at or above your limit. Hs is roughly the average height of the biggest third of the waves. Individual waves can be close to twice that.

Extremes

"1 sea state in 100 is above X m" means exactly that: 1 in every 100 of the 3 hourly records was higher than X. It is not a 100 year wave and should never be used as one. Design extremes need a proper extreme value study.

Weather windows

How many separate working periods of your chosen length, with Hs below your limit, a typical month holds. A 3 day calm counts as three 24 hour periods. It is estimated from how long calm spells usually last, so treat it as a planning figure. Checked against exact counts, it lands within about one period a month on average.

Across the day

Average wind and wave height in 3 hour slots of the local solar day, where noon is when the sun is highest, not clock time. Near coasts and in the tropics you often see a calm morning and a windier afternoon from the sea breeze. Out in the open ocean there is usually no daily pattern, and the explorer says so.

Current and tide

Typical peak tidal stream at springs and neaps, how long the slack lasts at each turn, and the background current that is there regardless of tide. The model gives an average over the water column, which is then scaled to the surface and seabed with the standard 1/7 power profile. The combined table adds tide, wind driven and background current the way DNVGL-RP-C205 section 4.1 describes. It is a screening figure, not a design current.

Tropical cyclones

How often tropical storms and cyclones have passed within 120, 250, 500 and 1,000 nautical miles of the point in your months, and the strongest one on record. Many operations start securing and demobilising when a cyclone comes within 1,000 nm, so this downtime is on top of what the wave statistics show.

Assets

Platforms, wells, fields and pipelines are shown from public registers so you can get your bearings. Positions are indicative only and must never be used for navigation.

3. What it can't tell you

  • Near the coast. The models are coarse and unreliable close to land, in bays, and in shallow or sheltered water.
  • Cyclone seas. Reanalysis smooths out tropical cyclones and tends to understate the worst seas.
  • Short events. Records are every 3 hours, so anything shorter can fall between them.
  • Your vessel. Real workability also depends on wave period, direction, current and how your vessel moves. A limit on Hs alone is only the first cut.
  • Design. None of this is for engineering design, navigation or go / no-go decisions.

Sea State Explorer is published by Thrust Maritime. Values are long term statistics for open water, from reanalysis model data. They are not forecasts, can understate extremes including tropical cyclones, and are unreliable near coasts and in sheltered or shallow water. Use them for early planning only, not for navigation, engineering design or go / no-go decisions. No warranty is given.
